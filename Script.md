Final Production Plan:“How LG Became LG”
Working title: The Spark That Became LG
Duration: Approximately 20 minutes
Narration target: 2,200–2,500 words
Format: 16:9, 4K or 1080p, 24 fps
Narration style: Deep, warm, cinematic, emotionally controlled
Visual journey: Muted historical tones → richer colours → clean modern imagery → optimistic futuristic visuals

Important: This is an original cinematic script inspired by the pacing of premium AI documentaries—not a copy of the referenced video. Historical product images, logos and dates should be verified and approved internally before final publication.


Part 1 — Final 20-Minute Script
0:00–1:30 — Opening Hook: A Signal in the Darkness
Visual direction

Black screen with the sound of distant wind
Post-war Korean streets
Damaged buildings and small workshops
Close-up of tired hands repairing machinery
A family gathered around a silent radio
An engineer switching on a handmade radio
Warm light slowly emerging from darkness

Narration

Every great transformation begins with a moment that appears insignificant.
A question asked in a small room.
A sketch drawn under a dim light.
A pair of hands refusing to accept that something cannot be done.
Long before LG became a name recognised around the world, Korea was rebuilding.
Resources were scarce.
Infrastructure was limited.
And many of the products people needed in their daily lives had to come from somewhere else.
But in that uncertainty, a different possibility began to emerge.
What if the things people depended on every day could be created here?
What if Korean hands could build products trusted by Korean families?
And what if one small company could help a nation imagine a different future?
This is not simply the history of televisions, refrigerators or washing machines.
It is the story of a belief.
A belief that technology should not exist only to demonstrate what is possible.
It should exist to make life better.
This is the story of Lucky.
The story of GoldStar.
And the story of how they became…
LG.

Music
A low piano pulse, distant strings and subtle industrial ambience. Introduce the main musical theme when “LG” is spoken.

1:30–4:00 — Chapter One: Trust Before Technology
On-screen chapter title
CHAPTER ONE — TRUST BEFORE TECHNOLOGY
Visual direction

Reconstruction of a modest 1940s Korean workshop
Ingredients being prepared by hand
Containers arranged on a wooden table
Lucky Cream and household-product imagery
Store shelves and families using everyday products
Founder represented respectfully, preferably by silhouette or archival material

Narration

The story begins not with electronics, but with everyday life.
In the late 1940s, founder Koo In-hwoi established the business that would begin the wider Lucky legacy.
Its early products belonged to the ordinary moments of life—personal care and household necessities.
They may not have looked revolutionary.
But they carried something essential.
Trust.
In those early years, quality was not treated as a technical specification written on a page.
Quality was a promise made to the customer.
If a product entered somebody’s home, it had to deserve its place there.
That principle would become more valuable than any single invention.
Because products change.
Markets change.
Technologies change.
But trust is what allows a company to continue moving forward.
The earliest Lucky products demonstrated that a Korean company could understand local customers, manufacture dependable goods and become part of people’s daily routines.
Yet the ambition growing inside the company was larger.
Korea did not only need consumer products.
It needed technological capability.
It needed engineers.
It needed factories.
And it needed proof that the future could be designed and built at home.
The next chapter would require a different kind of courage.
The courage to enter an industry where experience was limited…
and where failure would be visible to everyone.

LG’s official history connects founder Koo In-hwoi’s uncompromising approach to quality—from Lucky Cream to LG’s later home appliances—with the company’s continuing customer-focused philosophy. [lg.com], [lg.com]

4:00–7:00 — Chapter Two: A Nation Hears Its Future
On-screen chapter title
CHAPTER TWO — A NATION HEARS ITS FUTURE
Visual direction

GoldStar establishment
Engineers studying electronic components
Vacuum tubes, wires and an old circuit board
Reconstructed production line
Close-up of a radio dial
Radio illuminating on a table
Family listening together
Sound waves travelling across a Korean landscape

Narration

In 1958, GoldStar was established—the company that would eventually become LG Electronics.
Its mission was bold:
to build electronics in Korea, for Korea.
At the time, even a radio represented an enormous challenge.
Every circuit mattered.
Every component had to work.
Every mistake returned the engineers to the beginning.
Imagine the room.
A workbench covered with wires.
Technical drawings marked and corrected again and again.
Engineers working late into the night—not because success was guaranteed, but because it was not.
Then, in 1959, the effort produced a defining achievement:
Korea’s first domestically manufactured radio.
At first, there would have been only static.
Then a faint signal.
A voice.
Music.
News travelling through the air and entering a Korean home through a Korean-made device.
To the people listening, it was a radio.
To the people who built it, it was proof.
Proof that Korea could create advanced consumer technology.
Proof that local engineering could transform an ambitious idea into a working product.
And proof that one breakthrough could become the foundation for another.
In 1965 came Korea’s first domestically produced refrigerator.
In 1966, Korea’s first television.
The radio brought voices into the home.
The refrigerator changed how families preserved food.
The television opened a window to a larger world.
These were not merely machines.
They changed the rhythm of everyday life.
A family’s home was becoming more connected, more comfortable and more capable.
GoldStar was not simply manufacturing appliances.
It was helping create a new idea of modern living.

LG Electronics was founded as GoldStar in 1958, followed by Korea’s first domestically produced radio in 1959, refrigerator in 1965 and television in 1966. [lg.com], [lg.com]

In 1995, the company was rebranded as LG Electronics.
Decades of heritage were brought together under two letters:
L. G.
Simple enough to remember.
Strong enough to travel.
Human enough to become more than a corporate abbreviation.
The new identity represented transformation.
But it did not erase the past.
The Lucky commitment to customer trust remained.
The GoldStar courage to build what had not been built before remained.
A new name carried those values into a new era.
And over time, that identity became associated with a promise:
Life’s Good.
Those words were not only about ownership of a product.
They expressed something larger.
The best technology does not demand to become the centre of life.
It supports life.
It creates comfort without complication.
It brings people closer to experiences they value.
It gives time back.
The logo was new.
The world was changing.
But the purpose was remarkably familiar:
understand people…
and use innovation to make their lives better.

LG Electronics formally adopted the LG name in 1995. Its current “Life’s Good” brand promise focuses on making everyday experiences better through human-centred innovation. [lg.com], [lg.com]

The years that followed produced another series of turning points.
In 1998, LG introduced its Direct Drive motor technology for washing machines.
In 2001, it unveiled a refrigerator using an Inverter Linear Compressor.
In 2011 came the LG Styler, bringing steam-based garment care into a new product category.
In 2012, LG released an 84-inch Ultra HD television for retail sale.
In 2013, it mass-produced and launched a large-screen 55-inch OLED television.
Each milestone began as a technical challenge.
But customers did not experience a motor, compressor or display panel as an isolated piece of engineering.
They experienced quieter operation.
Better efficiency.
More convenient care for clothing.
Richer and more immersive images.
That is the hidden work of innovation.
Thousands of calculations…
years of development…
prototypes that never leave the laboratory…
all refined until complexity becomes a simple human experience.
Innovation appears dramatic on the day it is announced.
But its true impact happens later:
when it becomes so useful, so reliable and so familiar that people stop thinking of it as innovation at all.
Today, LG’s work reaches far beyond individual home appliances.
Screens have become platforms.
Vehicles have become intelligent environments.
Air solutions support homes, businesses and infrastructure.
Connected devices increasingly operate as part of larger systems.
The company that once helped place a radio in the Korean home is now working across the home, mobility, commercial spaces, platforms and business infrastructure.
The scale has changed.
The underlying question has not.
How can technology make this moment better?
